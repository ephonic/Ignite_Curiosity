# Source inconsistencies retained — chapters 066–085

These notes identify issues noticed during faithful translation, not a comprehensive independent revision. The English text retains the Chinese source's claims and qualifications; source fragments are numbered by chapter.

## Chapter 066 — Happiness

- The questionnaire is said to have no question stem immediately after its question is printed. The translation renders the intended contrast as no problem to solve, while preserving the single-question exercise.
- The Epicurus passage conflates the English term for hedonism with a word derived from his name. The translation retains its etymological claim as an English word for pleasure-seeking, without falsely deriving “hedonism” from Epicurus.
- Confucius and Aristotle are described as roughly contemporary; their lives are separated by over a century. Camus is grouped with existentialists without noting his own resistance to that label.
- The phrase rendered “others could not endure the hardship” has six Chinese characters, despite the source's explicit claim of five; the count remains unchanged.
- Income–happiness evidence is presented as a settled flattening curve, and Easterlin's paradox as established fact. The source does not discuss different income scales, well-being measures, or ongoing empirical disagreements.
- The peak–end rule is generalised to retrospective life evaluation and nearly replaces average experience. The travel comparison is illustrative, not a demonstrated universal ranking.
- Nozick is described as reporting a majority's refusal, without distinguishing a philosophical intuition from empirical survey evidence. The thought experiment's safety assumptions and counterarguments are fully retained.
- The claim that recommendation algorithms precisely calculate dopamine is rhetorical rather than a literal description of their measurement. Claims about stimulation thresholds and scrolling satisfaction are generalisations.
- National happiness rankings and Nordic policy explanations are source-present, undated descriptions. Bhutan's policy history and the scope of government welfare measurement are compressed; no new policy claims or current rankings have been introduced.

## Chapter 067 — Meaning

- The postcard's upper-right recipient-address placement and the inference that nobody but its finder knows of it are retained as narrated, despite the writer necessarily having known it.
- Religious traditions are compressed into single accounts of meaning. Kierkegaard's religious commitment and Camus's rejection of the existentialist label are not discussed in the source.
- The closing sentence of the three-level tool says “fourth-level collapse” belongs to the universe. This is a source numbering error, retained rather than silently replacing it with first level.
- A He's grief is categorically diagnosed as level-three collapse, and most existential crises are similarly generalised. These are the chapter's interpretive claims, not clinical diagnoses. The later explicit recommendation to seek professional support for persistent symptoms is fully retained.
- Frankl's observation about survival is presented without separating meaning's psychological role from the overwhelming material and arbitrary determinants of concentration-camp survival.
- Nihilism is treated as behaviourally impossible because people avoid danger and help friends. The source does not distinguish philosophical propositions about ultimate value from instinct, local preference, or varieties of nihilism.
- The three Chinese death passages represent selected traditions, as the source itself cautions, not a denial that other Chinese traditions posit afterlives. Zhuangzi's transformation narrative remains attributed and paraphrased.
- Death is called the only act no one can perform for another. This is the source's emphatic rendering of Heidegger, not an exhaustive literal classification of acts.
- Universal claims about actual grief rejecting philosophy and the origins of “empty-heart syndrome” are preserved as broad source assertions. The practical distinction between conceptual discussion and support for bereavement is retained.
- The final claim that all the chapter's philosophers warn against externally assigned meaning conflicts with the explicitly defended inherited and religious accounts earlier. Both sides remain present.

## Chapter 068 — After Rome

- The opening coin combines a profile portrait, helmet and spear, and Victory holding a cross around 500 without identifying a specific specimen. Universal recognition as currency, particularly in China, is stronger than archaeological evidence of coins or imitations in tombs alone establishes.
- Romulus Augustulus is called the final western emperor without distinguishing Julius Nepos's continuing recognised claim until 480. The bloodless Ravenna scene and unchanged peasant experience are reconstructions; the wider coup involved violence.
- The source first introduces four reconstructed voices, then provides five. Both the stated four and all five passages are retained.
- Medieval local security, manors, castles, churches, and monasteries are compressed into a broad post-476 process. Regional timing and institutional differences are not supplied.
- The legal definition is translated from the supplied Chinese alongside the exact Latin. Its attribution to Justinian's Institutes and Ulpian is retained.
- Tang evidence is introduced as the same period as 533, although Zhenguan belongs to the seventh century and the Old Book of Tang was compiled later. Its use to infer Chinese historians explicitly rejecting a western fall exceeds the cited diplomatic identification.
- “Nobody uses a dead government's currency as hard money” is a rhetorical generalisation; coins can continue circulating after an issuing regime disappears.
- Britain, literacy, tile production, roundhouses, and long-distance trade are presented in categorical compressed decline narratives, though the source also explicitly differentiates regions.
- Domesday's mill count does not by itself establish an unprecedented Roman comparison. The diffusion of ploughs, collars, and horseshoes, and dating-system invention in 525, simplify longer histories.
- The oldest surviving “complete” manuscripts of Cicero and Tacitus are generalised across different works and transmission histories. Greek-to-Arabic preservation is a source simplification, not an exclusive account of transmission.
- Baghdad's Islamic world appears within a fifth-to-tenth-century panorama despite Baghdad's later eighth-century foundation; the source's broad timeframe is retained. Arab expansion is explained largely through Byzantine–Sasanian exhaustion without claiming a comprehensive account in these notes.
- Petrarch is said to label his own age dark, while earlier passages claim nobody in the medieval millennium did so. The chapter's rhetoric about all period names being wholly retrospective also simplifies contemporaries' Renaissance consciousness.
- “Half” the darkness being lack of records and modern evidence growing fastest are unsourced rhetorical proportions. The final invention list groups developments from widely separated medieval centuries.

- Marco Polo's Alexandria comparison calls it Christendom's greatest port although the city was not then under Christian government. The source's warning not to read his hundredfold figure literally is retained.

- The Neo-Confucian description says it did not discuss supernatural matters, oversimplifying its cosmological and religious dimensions. The reform debate's absence of a true winner is an evaluative statement.
- Present-day port rankings and mobile-payment leadership are undated source claims. The internet/smartphone invention comparison reduces complex international histories to one American answer. No updated rankings or technology-origin claims have been added.

- Pahlavi is glossed as Old Persian in the source; this is retained although Middle Persian is the relevant linguistic designation.
- The Geniza letters' script/language relationship is reversed: the source says a community's language written in Arabic letters, whereas the relevant Judeo-Arabic documents commonly write Arabic in Hebrew script. The storage rationale is also generalised to all written paper.
- Córdoba is listed among nodes continuing after Baghdad's 1258 sack without noting its Christian conquest in 1236. Timbuktu and Ulugh Beg belong to later phases of the broad network, not the opening ninth-century scene.
- The equation x² + 10x = 39 is preserved in the source's plain-text notation, not silently recast as a new mathematical environment. The solution retains only the positive root, matching the historical/source treatment; unrestricted modern real solving also yields −13. “Whatever the numbers” generalises a positive-coefficient procedure.
- The claim that the example is the book's first equation type and the blanket contrast with all Greek equation-solving are source simplifications. Al-Khwarizmi's exact birthplace and institutional role are presented more certainly than the account establishes.
- Translation-preservation claims concerning most Galen and portions of Aristotle compress differing transmission histories. Novelty claims for algebra, experimental optics, and Earth measurement do not separate earlier antecedents from specific innovations.
- Mongol networks are contrasted categorically with Islamic ones, although later paragraphs show Mongol/post-Mongol continuities. The narration of nineteenth-century civilisation moving west-to-east is retained despite its tension with the earlier relay direction.
- Persian's withdrawal from scholarship into poetry, compulsory Arabic thought, and most languages' inability to produce a physics paper are sweeping source claims, not literal linguistic incapacity established by the translation.
- The source's explicit cautions about the Talas papermaking story, Du Huan not naming papermakers, weak or fabricated China-seeking hadith, unequal network power, slavery, and women's underrepresentation remain fully present.

- Rinan is placed roughly in northern Vietnam in the source, though the historical commandery extended in central Vietnam. The Book of Han compilation date and Huangzhi's identification are simplified; the quotation's ellipses and original itinerary names are retained.

- Braudel's slow structures are presented as largely unchanged by events and prohibitions affecting price only. The source later supplies an explicit caution against determinism, fully retained.

- The final claim that a master's order issues tickets to everyone is in tension with the earlier pass system, violence, and unequal access. It is preserved as part of the source's concluding debate, not endorsed as an established historical outcome.
