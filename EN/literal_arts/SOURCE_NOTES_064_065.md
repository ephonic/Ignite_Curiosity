# Potential source inconsistencies: chapters 64–65

These notes record possible problems noticed while translating the supplied
Chinese chapters. They are not a comprehensive independent fact-check. Claims,
examples, arguments, and quotations remain source-faithful; philosophical and
legal descriptions are not current professional guidance. Line numbers refer
to the supplied Chinese chapter fragments. Quotations are translated from those
fragments rather than substituted from published English editions. Optional
short navigation titles do not replace the full visible headings.

## Chapter 64 — Equality and giving each their due

- Lines 5 and 43 describe seven family members but later refer to dividing the
  cake into eight equal slices. Line 18 dates the classroom scene to late May
  while describing June heat. Line 132 says the teacher considered the matter
  for two days and announced the result on Wednesday after the Friday meeting.
  These narrative inconsistencies are retained.
- Line 51 calls fairness potentially a “sixth” standard after introducing four
  standards. The ordinal is retained rather than silently changed.
- Lines 26 and 66 move between rewarding the most improved students and giving
  resources to the best students; these are not identical criteria. The
  classroom, family, and school-assistance scenarios are illustrative, without
  identified underlying case records.
- Lines 9–11 present “I cut, you choose” and the cutter choosing last as a
  self-enforcing mechanism for the whole family. The example does not establish
  all the strategic and preference assumptions needed for that extension.
- Lines 82–86 generalize about Hadza sharing, Athenian selection by lot, and
  contemporary jury selection. Citizenship exclusions and variation among
  communities and institutions are not developed in the source.
- Lines 110–129 use broad ancient dates and make a priority claim about the
  Book of Rites and welfare provision. The section heading “three thousand
  years ago” does not precisely date all three sources. Analects interpretations
  and Hammurabi's status categories are simplified; “free man” is retained.
- Lines 140–156 compress libertarianism, utilitarianism, egalitarianism, and the
  capability approach into competing classroom explanations. In particular,
  Nozick's position is reduced to a lawful procedure and treated as neglecting
  the starting point, despite the same paragraph mentioning legitimate initial
  acquisition. Sen's famine analysis and the portrayal of a tablet without
  Wi-Fi as half a brick are source simplifications, not independent findings.
- Lines 158–171 treat several theories as sharing a social-contract ancestor,
  portray the veil of ignorance as random assignment of identities, and state
  Rawls's difference principle as improvement over complete equality. That
  comparison does not by itself express maximization of the least advantaged
  position. Risk aversion, Sen's objection, and communitarian criticism are
  compressed paraphrases, retained as the source's teaching framework.
- Lines 176–180 discuss aid disclosure, school admissions, housing, and delivery
  algorithms in general terms without naming jurisdictions or platforms.
  Medical triage and claims of eye damage illustrate the argument; they are
  not diagnoses or a clinical guide. The father's dietary restriction is the
  advice assigned to his doctor within the fictional cake example.

## Chapter 65 — The state's right to command

- Lines 3–7 describe a one-yuan coin as stainless steel, with a chrysanthemum
  and denomination on one face and the national emblem on the other. This
  combination may conflate coin designs or issues; the unspecified mint year,
  composition, metal value, and bun price need verification. The currency unit
  “fen” preserves the source's Chinese minor unit rather than implying U.S.
  cents. The blanket legal-tender and counterfeiting descriptions are source
  claims, not updated legal advice.
- Lines 14–28 reconstruct an unnamed English village in 1644. The requisitions,
  market closure, school closure, and miller's family are illustrative, not
  documented individual events. Hobbes's departure chronology also needs
  qualification: line 113 says he had just escaped civil-war England.
- Lines 37–43 conflate legitimacy with a kind of obedience. Normative entitlement
  to rule and people's belief that rule is entitled are related but distinct;
  the chapter later discusses Weber's sociological account.
- Lines 75–85 paraphrase Mencius and the Daodejing, generalize about emperors
  seeking textual changes, and compare Daoist government with minimal modern
  government. These interpretive and historical claims are source assertions.
- Lines 92–106 simplify statehood, recognition, nations, and governments.
  Switzerland's four languages have differentiated legal status; language
  communities do not automatically constitute separate nations. The Kurdish
  example needs distinctions among sovereign statehood, autonomous government,
  language diversity, and identity. British institutional continuity and the
  number of government changes are broad rhetoric, not a constitutional audit.
- Lines 113–126 compress Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau into hypothetical contracts.
  Hobbes's surrender of all violent rights leaves self-preservation qualifications
  undeveloped; Locke's composition chronology, the Glorious Revolution's
  bloodlessness, and the colonists' “direct copying” are simplified. General
  will is not independently established here as identical to actual consensus.
- Lines 144–146 present Socrates's trial as wholly lawful and almost universally
  condemned by later observers. Charges, jury numbers, Plato's literary account,
  and the language of poison drinking are compressed; the claim to the oldest
  evidence of rights limiting majorities is not independently substantiated.
- Lines 148–156 define civil disobedience through publicity, nonviolence, and
  acceptance of punishment, then summarize Thoreau, Gandhi, and King. These
  are historical/philosophical examples, not procedural advice. The contrast
  between natural law and positivism is unusually stark: positivism is reduced
  to proper enactment, and the assertion that only natural law supplies a
  vocabulary for condemning atrocities overlooks other moral criticisms.
  Nuremberg's treatment of superior orders is likewise summarized broadly.
- Lines 161–169 discuss class votes, app agreements, data rights, and school
  phone inspections without specifying legal jurisdictions. Their purposes
  are conceptual comparison and inquiry into legitimacy, limits, and remedies,
  not statements of universally enforceable present-day rights.
